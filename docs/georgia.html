Georgia: a closer look

A fifty-state index is a blunt tool. Georgia is where I live, so this pairs the project's score with Georgia's real figures from the EIA and asks what actually sits behind them.

Where Georgia sits in the index

In this project's ranking Georgia comes out near the very top, and the reason is specific: not its structure, but how much interruption customers actually see.

The index scores Georgia high mainly on outage burden (the SAIDI and SAIFI reliability measures), and only middling on concentration and spare capacity. That is a Southeast pattern: storms and heat drive interruptions more than the mix of plants does. These figures come from the project, which runs on sample data, so read them as illustrative.

The big plants

Georgia's grid leans on a small number of very large stations, which is exactly the kind of single-point concentration the index tries to pick up. Its five biggest, from eGRID:

Plant Vogtle (nuclear)

Vogtle's two new reactors, Units 3 and 4, came online in 2023 and 2024, the first newly built US reactors in a generation. The capacity above is the 2023 figure, before Unit 4 was running; with it, Vogtle is around 4,500 MW and the largest nuclear plant in the country. That is a big block of steady, low-carbon baseload, which eases the exposure-deficit side of the score even as it deepens the reliance on one site.

Plant Scherer (coal)

Scherer, near Juliette, is one of the largest coal-fired plants in the United States, and at 8.3 million tons of CO2 in 2023 one of the bigger single emitters too. Coal's share has been falling in Georgia as gas and nuclear grow, but a plant this size still shapes both the emissions picture and how much rides on a single asset.

Most of the state is served by Georgia Power, a Southern Company utility, alongside a large set of electric membership cooperatives and municipal systems.

The storms behind the outages

If the index flags Georgia on outage burden, the obvious question is why, and a second open source answers it. NOAA's Storm Events Database records the severe weather that actually knocks the power out, and Georgia gets plenty: hurricanes and tropical systems tracking up from the Gulf and Atlantic, along with summer thunderstorms and the odd ice storm.

The clearest recent case is Hurricane Helene in September 2024, which pushed inland across Georgia and left well over a million customers without power, one of the worst outage events in the state's history. That is the kind of event the SAIDI and SAIFI figures are quietly averaging, and it is why Georgia's exposure here is about weather far more than about its fleet of plants.

What it means for resilience

Putting the two halves together: the index flags Georgia on outage burden, which fits a storm-exposed Southeast far more than it reflects a fragile fleet. Structurally the state is actually fairly well placed. It sells a huge amount of power, keeps prices middling, and has just added a large block of firm nuclear that pushes back on the capacity-margin risk.

The real watch-item is concentration: a grid resting on a few very large plants is efficient but has more to lose from any single outage or forced retirement. That is the thread a Georgia-specific version of this project would pull on next, using plant-level data (EPA's eGRID, or the HIFLD infrastructure layers), the storm record, and county population, rather than the sample.

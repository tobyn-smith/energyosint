Grid Resilience Exposure Index

Which US states look most exposed to losing power, and why the answer differs by state. Built from public data, for anyone who has to think about energy policy rather than wiring.

Tobyn Smith / built from open EIA, EPA and NOAA data

Move through it with the arrow keys, the marks along the bottom, or a swipe on a phone. Outage figures and plant capacity are real; only peak demand is still a sample, and it is labelled wherever it matters.

Why look at this

Keeping the lights on is quietly one of the larger infrastructure questions in US policy, and several pressures are pulling on the grid at the same time.

1

Weather

More frequent and more severe storms, heat and cold snaps are behind most of the large outages.

2

An ageing network

A lot of the wires and substations were built decades ago and are being asked to carry more.

3

A changing fuel mix

Coal is retiring, gas and renewables are growing, and that shifts where the weak spots sit.

Most of the data that speaks to this is already public, but it is scattered across agencies and formats, so the obstacle is not just what gets measured but how little of it is ever brought together. What this project does is pull a few of those sources into one comparable score per state.

What it is built on

Three public inputs, from two different federal agencies. No paywalls, no private feeds, and nothing that needs a login.

1

How often the power fails

The industry reliability measures, SAIDI and SAIFI, for how long and how often customers lose power.

2

How the supply is built

Generating capacity by plant and fuel, from EPA's eGRID, which shows how much a state leans on one fuel or one big station.

3

How much slack there is

Installed capacity set against peak demand, so you can see how thin the margin is on a bad day.

The outage figures are real: EIA-861 for 2023, every state. Capacity and peak demand still come from a built-in sample, so the project runs for anyone without a data key. That mix is worth keeping in mind when reading the ranking.

Turning that into one number

Every state gets a score from 0 to 100, where higher means it looks more likely to struggle to keep the power on. The number is built from three pieces.

1

Outage burden

How often the power goes out and for how long, from the SAIDI and SAIFI measures.

2

Concentration

How much of a state's power leans on one fuel type or a single very large plant.

3

Exposure deficit

Whether there is much spare capacity above peak demand, and how varied the supply is.

The three are put on the same scale and then blended. The weights live in a single settings file, so they are easy to change; the next two slides let you do exactly that.

The same score can mean different things

This is the part worth carrying into a policy conversation. A high score is not one problem but three: what matters is which of the pieces is doing the work, and that changes from state to state.

1

Mississippi and West Virginia: one fuel

Mississippi takes 84 per cent of its capacity from gas and West Virginia 83 per cent from coal, the two most concentrated supplies in the country. They lead the table on that alone. The worry is what happens to a state that has bet almost everything on one thing.

2

Maine: the opposite problem

Maine has the least concentrated fuel mix of any state and still comes third, because its customers lost thirty one hours of power in 2023. Nothing there is about fragile plants, so the lever is hardening lines and restoring faster.

3

Same score, different job

That is the point. Spreading a fuel mix, hardening lines and adding spare capacity are three different pieces of policy, with different costs and different timescales. Because of this, a ranking on its own tells you far less than the ranking read alongside its driver.

The driver label under each state on the map is the shorthand for this.

What this is not

A few honest limits. For a policy reader these arguably matter more than the ranking itself, so they are here rather than buried at the end.

1

It is not operational

State-level figures blur a lot. A state can look fine overall and still have fragile pockets inside it.

2

One of the three parts is still sampled

Outage figures and plant capacity are both real now. Peak demand is not, so the spare-capacity part is still a worked example. The parts are labelled wherever they appear.

3

It leaves out the wires

The score says nothing about the transmission network that moves power around, which probably matters as much as the plants do.

As a result the very top and the very bottom are the parts worth reading. The middle should be treated loosely rather than quoted.

Testing it against something else

The index leans hardest on outages, so it is worth checking that data against a source collected by somebody else entirely. NOAA's Storm Events Database is compiled by the National Weather Service and has nothing to do with what utilities file to the EIA.

1

A test that can fail

The EIA publishes outage minutes twice, counting major storm days and excluding them. The gap between the two is the part storms account for. If the weather story is right, that gap should track how much damaging weather a state actually had.

2

It largely does not

Across the fifty places that report both figures, everywhere bar Hawaii, the rank correlation is 0.21 for how many damaging storms a state had and 0.24 for how much damage they did. Texas and Georgia record the most storm events in the country and lose few minutes. Maine records a fraction as many and lost the most of anyone.

3

Which is the interesting part

Storms obviously matter, or the two outage figures would not be so far apart. But how much bad weather arrives does not decide who suffers most. What the grid is like when it arrives, and how fast crews restore it, look like they matter more.

That is an argument against treating exposure as fate, and it cuts in favour of spending on hardening and restoration. It is one year of data, so I would not lean on it harder than that.

The view from abroad

This is a US project, but the question is not a US question, and reading it from an international angle changes what counts as normal in the first place.

1

The gap is real

In much of western Europe a typical customer loses power for minutes a year. In the US the median state lost four hours in 2023 once major storms are counted, and Maine, the worst, lost thirty one. Countries measure this differently, so the figures do not line up neatly, but the gap is too wide to be a measurement artefact.

2

Different systems, different trade-offs

Countries that perform well tend to bury more of their lines and pay for it in their bills. The US runs long overhead lines through forest and hurricane country, which is cheaper right up until the weather arrives.

3

The method travels

Nothing in the scoring is US-specific. Given capacity, reliability and demand data, the same three components would run on German Länder or Indian states. The hard part abroad is the data, not the method.

That is the quiet upside of the open-data picture: the US publishes enough for anyone to rebuild all of this from scratch, which not many countries do.

A closer look at Georgia

To show what a fuller version looks like, I set the score for one state against its actual figures from the EIA, EPA eGRID and NOAA, including an interactive map of every power plant of 50 MW and up that you can filter by fuel.

On the real data Georgia lands mid-table, nineteenth of fifty-one, which is a duller answer than the sample gave and a more useful one. Its 343 minutes off supply in 2023 sit close to the national middle, and its fuel mix is broad enough that concentration is not what pushes it up.

How it was built, and how to run it

The analysis runs in Python: it pulls the public data (or falls back to a built-in sample), cleans and joins it, scores each state, and writes the charts and a results table. The map is drawn in R with the usmap package, and the same results export as a GeoPackage so they open in mapping software like QGIS.

Built with pandas, matplotlib, ggplot2 and geopandas. With Python installed, from the project folder it is two commands:

# install the libraries, then run the pipeline
pip install -r requirements.txt
python pipeline.py

Full setup notes, including the optional R map and how to plug in a live EIA data key, are in the project README.

Data: EIA forms 860 and 861, EPA eGRID, NOAA storm records. Code and the full write-up are on GitHub.

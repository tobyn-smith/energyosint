Georgia: a closer look

Georgia is where I live, so I wanted to look at it more closely than a fifty-state ranking lets you. This puts the project's score for Georgia next to the state's real numbers from the EIA and a couple of other open sources.

Where Georgia sits in the index

On the real outage figures Georgia sits in the middle of the table, and what pushes it up is how often the power goes out rather than how its system is built.

The big plants

A lot of Georgia's power comes from a handful of very big stations, which is the sort of thing the concentration part of the score is meant to catch. The five largest, from eGRID:

Plant Vogtle (nuclear)

Vogtle's two new reactors, Units 3 and 4, started up in 2023 and 2024, the first newly built US reactors in a generation. The number above is for 2023, before Unit 4 was running; with it Vogtle is about 4,500 MW, the largest nuclear plant in the country. All that steady, low-carbon output helps on the spare-capacity side of the score. It also means a lot now rides on one site.

Plant Scherer (coal)

Scherer, near Juliette, is one of the biggest coal plants in the country, and at 8.3 million tons of CO2 in 2023 one of the larger single emitters as well. Coal is a shrinking part of Georgia's power now, but a plant this size still counts, both for emissions and for how much sits on one asset.

Most of the state buys from Georgia Power, part of Southern Company, with the rest covered by a big set of electric membership co-ops and city-owned systems.

The storms behind the outages

Outage burden is still the biggest part of Georgia's score, so what sits behind it? A second open source helps here. NOAA's Storm Events Database logs the severe weather that takes the power down, and Georgia gets a lot of it: hurricanes and tropical storms coming up from the Gulf and the Atlantic, plus summer thunderstorms and the odd ice storm.

The clearest recent example is Hurricane Helene in September 2024. It tracked inland across Georgia and knocked out power to well over a million customers, one of the worst outage events the state has had. That is the sort of thing the SAIDI and SAIFI numbers are averaging out, and it is why Georgia's exposure here is really about the weather, not its plants.

What it means for resilience

Put the two halves together and it holds up, though less dramatically than the sample suggested. Georgia's exposure is mostly about outage burden, which suits a storm-prone Southeast more than it says anything is wrong with its plants. The levers for that are unglamorous: trimming trees, hardening lines, getting crews out faster. On the structural side the state looks fairly solid: it makes plenty of power, prices are ordinary, and the new nuclear at Vogtle adds a big block of firm capacity.

The thing I would keep an eye on is concentration. Leaning on a few very large plants is efficient, but there is more to lose if one of them trips or gets retired. That is where a proper Georgia version of this would go next: real plant-level data (EPA's eGRID or the HIFLD infrastructure layers), the storm record, and county population, instead of the sample.
